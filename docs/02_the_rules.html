⚙️ The Two Rules: c() and t()

Every single thing in the universe — particles, forces, space, time — comes from two mathematical rules. Let's understand each one deeply.

Why Complex Numbers?

Normal numbers can only tell you how much. Complex numbers tell you how much AND in which direction. They're the natural language for anything that can interfere — like waves.

🎯 Simple version — complex numbers

Imagine you're giving someone directions. Saying "walk 5 steps" is a regular number. Saying "walk 5 steps north-east" is a complex number — it has both a size (5) and a direction (north-east).

In physics, a "wave amplitude" is a complex number: it has a size (how strong) and a phase (where in its cycle). Two waves can cancel if their phases are opposite — that's destructive interference. This cancellation is the core of quantum mechanics, and it only works with complex numbers.

Rule 1: c() — The Propagation Function

🎯 Simple version — what c() does

Think of c() like a rumour spreading at school. When you hear something, you don't just pass it to the person next to you — you pass it to everyone at once, but with different strengths and slightly different versions (different complex amplitudes). Most versions cancel out (interference). Only the versions that add up survive — those become the "particle" you detect.

What it does

c() takes a configuration — a snapshot of information at one node — and outputs complex-valued amplitudes for all possible next configurations.

✓

Complex linear: Amplitudes use complex numbers, allowing interference

✓

Non-local: c() can reach any other node, not just adjacent ones

✓

Atemporal: All of c() runs "at once" — it's not limited by the speed of light

✗

Not reversible by itself: New nodes are created, old ones stay

What it creates

c() builds the entire quantum mechanical structure automatically:

✓

Wave functions — because amplitudes are complex

✓

Superposition — because c() can point to multiple nodes

✓

Interference — because complex amplitudes add/cancel

✓

Entanglement — because two nodes can share c() specifications

c: Config → ℂ[Config] c() maps each configuration to complex amplitudes on all configurations — this IS the quantum wave function

Each node sends complex-amplitude "tendrils" outward. Where many tendrils overlap and reinforce: a particle "resolves" (is observed) there.

Rule 2: t() — The Transformation Function

🎯 Simple version — what t() does

Think of t() like a perfect password manager. Every password (input) maps to exactly one encrypted version (output), and every encrypted version can only come from one password. Nothing is lost, nothing is duplicated. This "nothing lost" property is why information is conserved — and from that, all of physics follows.

Here's the wild part: just because t() never loses information, the universe must have an arrow of time. Once you've resolved a configuration (like measuring an electron), you can't un-resolve it. The past is locked in. That's the 2nd law of thermodynamics, falling out of one rule.

The single most important constraint: t() is bijective — every input maps to exactly one output, and every output comes from exactly one input. Information is never created or destroyed.

In quantum mechanics language: t() is unitary. This single property gives you:

t†t = 𝟙

The adjoint of t() times t() equals identity. This is the mathematical definition of "unitary." It directly causes energy conservation (via Noether's theorem applied to the time-translation symmetry this creates).

S = -Tr[ρ log ρ]

The von Neumann entropy formula is forced by this alone. The entropy formula isn't assumed — it's the unique formula satisfying unitarity + additivity for independent systems + continuity. This is a real mathematical theorem.

dS/dt ≥ 0

Entropy always increases — the 2nd law of thermodynamics — because t() is irreversible in the direction of resolution. Once a configuration is "observed," it can't be unobserved. The arrow of time emerges from this alone.
