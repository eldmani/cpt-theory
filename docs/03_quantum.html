What's Weird About Quantum Mechanics?

In classical physics, a ball is either here or there. In quantum mechanics, a particle can be in a superposition — a mix of both — until you measure it. The famous double-slit experiment shows this: an electron can go through both slits at once.

Why does nature work this way? In the Standard Model, this is just assumed. CPT derives it.

Complex Numbers Are the Key

Remember Axiom 1: the propagator c() is complex linear. This means c() works with complex numbers — numbers with a real part AND an imaginary part.

🎯 Simple version — why complex = quantum

Normal probability adds like this: P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B). If there's a 30% chance of rain OR snow, you add the chances. But quantum amplitudes are complex numbers, and they can subtract. In the double-slit experiment, the amplitude for "electron goes through slit 1 and hits spot X" plus the amplitude for "goes through slit 2 and hits spot X" can be zero — even though both are non-zero separately. That's why the screen has dark bands. No complex numbers = no dark bands = no quantum mechanics.

If c() were real linear

Classical physics

c(a + b) = c(a) + c(b) over real numbers. States add, but can't "interfere" in interesting ways. You get classical probability — just like mixing paint colors.

Since c() is complex linear

Quantum mechanics emerges

c(a + b) = c(a) + c(b) over complex numbers. Complex amplitudes add — and when you square them to get probability, cross-terms appear. This IS interference. This IS quantum mechanics.

Two complex propagators add together. The result (right) shows the characteristic interference pattern of quantum mechanics — bright and dark fringes. This is NOT put in by hand; it follows from c() being complex linear.

Stone's Theorem: From Propagation to Schrödinger

This is where CPT achieves something remarkable. There is a fundamental theorem in mathematics called Stone's Theorem:

🎯 Simple version — Stone's theorem

Imagine a perfectly smooth spinning wheel. If it spins at a constant rate (no jerks, no slowing), then you can describe its position at any time with a simple formula: angle = ω×t. Stone's theorem is the same idea for quantum evolution: if the universe evolves smoothly and unitarily (no information loss), then the evolution must have the form e^(-iHt) for some H. That H is the Hamiltonian — the energy operator. Differentiate it and you get the Schrödinger equation. No postulate needed.

Stone's Theorem (1932): If you have a group of linear operators {U(t)} that act on a complex vector space, and U(t) is continuous in t and preserves inner products (unitary), then there must exist a self-adjoint operator H such that:

U(t) = e^{-iHt}
1

The resolution front moves forward in time

The bijective time function t() defines a group of evolution operators — one for each moment. The universe at time t₂ is obtained by applying t() to the universe at t₁.

2

These operators act on a complex vector space

Since c() is complex linear, the space of all configurations v is a complex vector space. The evolution operators are complex-linear operators on this space.

3

The evolution preserves the inner product

The total "number" of configurations is conserved — nothing is created or destroyed by the propagation. This means the evolution is unitary: U(t)†U(t) = I.

4

Stone's theorem fires

A continuous one-parameter unitary group on a complex Hilbert space must take the form U(t) = e^{-iHt} for some Hermitian H. This is a proven mathematical fact.

5

The Schrödinger equation appears

Taking the derivative: d|ψ⟩/dt = -iH|ψ⟩, which is exactly iℏ d|ψ⟩/dt = H|ψ⟩. The Schrödinger equation is not an axiom — it's a theorem.

U(t) = e^{-iHt} [from Stone's Theorem]
d|ψ(t)⟩/dt = -iH|ψ(t)⟩
iℏ d|ψ(t)⟩/dt = H|ψ(t)⟩ [Schrödinger equation] ℏ just sets the units — it enters as the scale of the complex Hilbert space

What Quantum Mechanics Actually IS in CPT

🎯 Simple version — the big picture

Standard quantum mechanics hands you a list of rules and says "just accept them." The rules include: particles have wave functions (why?), wave functions evolve by the Schrödinger equation (why?), when you measure, the wave function collapses (why? how?).

CPT's answer: most of these rules are consequences of just two properties — c() uses complex numbers, and t() is bijective. The Schrödinger equation is a theorem, not a postulate. The uncertainty principle follows from Fourier analysis of complex functions. Entanglement is a consequence of c() connecting multiple nodes. The only thing still murky is measurement collapse — that's still a conjecture in CPT, same as everywhere else in physics.
